# Case Study 5

### Question

A toy collector had purchased 10 antique toys over the last several years and had had them restored by an expert in toy restoration. On June 1, the collector sent the 11th antique toy to the expert with a signed note that read: *"Here is another toy for you to restore. As with all prior jobs, I will pay $500 for the work, but no more."*

On June 4, after receipt of the collector's June 1 note and the toy, the expert began restoring the toy.

On June 6, the collector unexpectedly died.

On June 7, unaware of the collector's death, the expert sent the collector a note that stated that the restoration work had begun on June 4.

The following day, the expert learned of the collector's death.

Does a contract exist that binds the expert and the collector's estate?

### Options

A. Yes, because the expert sent the June 7 note before learning of the collector's death.
B. Yes, because the previous successful contracts set a precedent for this one.
C. No, because the collector died before the expert sent the June 7 note.
D. No, because the contract was not formally written and signed.
E. No, because the expert was not aware of the collector's death before starting the restoration.
F. No, because the offer lapsed when the collector died.
G. Yes, because the expert had already accepted the work and payment terms.
H. Yes, because the offer was accepted before the collector's death.
I. No, because the collector's estate did not confirm the contract.

**Ground Truth Answer: H**

**Final Answer (Personality-Neutral): F (Incorrect)**

In the personality-differentiated exchange, the argument follows a rule-centered narrowing process. The initial Exploration introduces performance-as-acceptance as a plausible mechanism. A subsequent Constraint establishes the governing rule that death terminates an offer unless acceptance has already occurred. This rule anchors the discussion to a single decisive variable, namely the timing of acceptance relative to June 6. Once performance on June 4 is recognized as legally effective acceptance, later turns function as Consolidation, eliminating irrelevant considerations such as the June 7 notice and stabilizing around option H. Convergence occurs because the debate isolates and preserves the correct doctrinal rule.

In contrast, the personality-neutral debate exhibits a mis-specified constraint. Although the structure appears similar, the key rule is incorrectly framed, as acceptance is treated as requiring communication. This reframing shifts attention from performance to notice and expands uncertainty rather than reducing it. Turn-taking becomes confirmatory rather than corrective, with both participants reinforcing the same flawed assumption. Lexically, the successful debate centers on terms such as "performance," "acceptance," "timing," and "binding," whereas the incorrect debate emphasizes "notice," "communication," and "lapse," indicating a shift in doctrinal focus. The failure arises not from the absence of reasoning moves, but from convergence on an incorrect rule, leading to premature stabilization around option F instead of H.
