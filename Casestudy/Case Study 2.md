# Case Study 2

### Question

Rawls claims that students with fewer native assets (such as intelligence) should be given:

### Options

A. the same level of attention and resources as those with more native assets.
B. educational resources based on their social background.
C. resources based on their willingness to learn.
D. educational resources based on their economic background.
E. less attention and fewer resources than those with more native assets.
F. entirely different educational resources than those with more native assets.
G. virtually no educational resources.
H. the opportunity to compete with those having more native assets.
I. more attention and resources than those with more native assets.
J. access to only basic education.

**Ground Truth Answer: I**

------

## Personality-Differentiated Debate (ESTJ vs. ESTP) — ❌ Incorrect

| Round | Dialogue                                                     |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1     | **[ESTJ \| Exploration]** Rawls' difference principle holds that inequalities are justified only if they benefit the least advantaged. Students with fewer native assets should therefore receive additional institutional support. **Answer: I** <br /> **[ESTP \| Constraint]** That assumes fairness alone determines allocation. But resources are limited—what concrete outcome justifies giving "more" rather than ensuring efficiency? |
| 2     | **[ESTJ \| Exploration]** If institutions must function effectively, we need criteria that can be systematically implemented. Economic background provides a measurable proxy for disadvantage. **Answer: D** <br /> **[ESTP \| Constraint]** Right—native assets are abstract and hard to quantify. Economic background is administratively verifiable and policy-ready. |
| 3     | **[ESTJ \| Constraint]** Given enforceability and scalability as institutional invariants, allocation must rely on operational criteria rather than philosophical abstraction. **Answer: D** <br /> **[ESTP \| Exploration]** If policy prioritizes measurable disadvantage, then D offers a clearer mechanism for distributing resources efficiently. |
| 4     | **[ESTJ \| Consolidation]** Therefore, under practical institutional constraints, economic background stabilizes as the most coherent allocation rule. **Answer: D** <br /> **[ESTP \| Consolidation]** Agreed—when prioritizing feasibility and outcome tracking, D becomes the defensible choice. |

**Final Answer (Personality-Conditioned): D (Incorrect)**

------

## Personality-Neutral Debate — ✅ Correct

| Round | Dialogue                                                     |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1     | **[Proponent \| Exploration]** Rawls argues that natural talents are morally arbitrary. If students are disadvantaged by native assets, justice requires compensatory support. **Answer: I** <br /> **[Opponent \| Constraint]** But does Rawls require "more resources," or merely equal opportunity to compete? |
| 2     | **[Proponent \| Exploration]** Equal opportunity is insufficient if structural disadvantages persist. The difference principle permits inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged. **Answer: I** <br /> **[Opponent \| Exploration]** If "least advantaged" includes natural disadvantages, compensatory allocation beyond equal treatment becomes justified. |
| 3     | **[Proponent \| Constraint]** Holding Rawls’ principle constant, institutions must counterbalance morally arbitrary disadvantages rather than simply treat everyone the same. **Answer: I** <br /> **[Opponent \| Consolidation]** Under that invariant interpretation, I aligns more directly with Rawls than options tied only to economic status. |
| 4     | **[Proponent \| Consolidation]** Therefore, Rawls’ framework supports allocating more attention and resources to those with fewer native assets. **Answer: I** <br /> **[Opponent \| Consolidation]** Yes, I most accurately reflects the compensatory logic embedded in the difference principle. |

**Final Answer (Personality-Neutral): I (Correct)**



In this Rawls case, the personality-conditioned debate between ESTJ and ESTP shows a competitive yet pragmatically convergent structure. The ESTJ proponent begins with a principle-driven Exploration, grounding the argument in Rawlsian fairness and compensation for morally arbitrary disadvantages. The ESTP opponent immediately introduces Constraint by questioning practical effectiveness and resource limitations, shifting the focus from normative justice to implementability. In response, the ESTJ strategically pivots from the original interpretation of "native assets" (I) to a more administratively measurable criterion (D), emphasizing institutional feasibility as the new organizing principle. The debate therefore moves from moral theory toward policy pragmatism and quickly reaches Consolidation around option D. Turn-taking is assertive and sequential. Each ESTP intervention narrows the range of acceptable justifications, and each ESTJ response reorganizes the proposal to remain coherent under these constraints. Lexically, the discussion shifts from abstract moral terms such as "justice," "fairness," and "systematic disadvantage" toward technocratic language including "measurable," "implementable," "scalable," and "track outcomes." This shift indicates a movement away from normative reasoning toward administrative realism.

In contrast, the personality-neutral debate follows a principle-stabilizing structure rather than a strategic pivot. The proponent introduces Rawls’ argument about moral arbitrariness through Exploration, and the opponent applies Constraint by questioning whether equal opportunity (H) might be sufficient. Instead of abandoning the original claim, the proponent reinforces the Rawlsian principle that inequalities are justified only if they benefit the least advantaged. This move tightens the conceptual boundary rather than changing the evaluative criterion. Turn-taking is more symmetric and less adversarial, and constraints function as clarification rather than pressure. Lexical patterns remain consistently normative, with recurring references to the "difference principle," the "least advantaged," and the "natural lottery." There is little shift toward administrative or instrumental language.
